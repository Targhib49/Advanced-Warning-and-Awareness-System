AWAS · Advanced Warning & Awareness System

What to learn, and why it matters here

Eight tracks, ordered so the parts you need to defend the project come before the parts you need to extend it. Every track names the file it unlocks, so nothing here is abstract — you can read the topic, then go read the code that uses it.

New to Python Start at Track 1 and do it properly. Everything else assumes it.
Comfortable in Python Skim Track 1 for the idioms this codebase uses, then start at Track 2.
Already trained a model Start at Track 4, then Tracks 3 and 7 — the domain science and the research method are what judges probe.

Before anything else

Ninety minutes, everyone, whatever your level. You cannot learn the parts without knowing what the whole is for.

02

Images, landmarks and the eye aspect ratio

~1 week

An image is a NumPy array of numbers. A face mesh is 478 points that a neural network places on a face. EAR is a ratio between six of those points. That is genuinely the whole of Layer 1's geometry — and understanding why EAR breaks under head rotation is the thing that makes the rest of our design make sense.

Unlocks awas/driver/ear.py, awas/driver/landmarks.py
  • DocsOpenCV-Python tutorials — "Gui Features" and "Core Operations" only. Skip the rest for now.
  • DocsMediaPipe Face Landmarker — what the model gives you and in what coordinate system.
  • CourseKaggle Learn: Computer Vision — free, short, hands-on.
  • PaperSoukupová & Čech (2016) — the EAR formula, four pages. See the reading list below.
03

The sleep science — where PERCLOS comes from

~1 week

This is the track most teams skip and the one judges probe hardest. PERCLOS is not something we invented: it is a validated measure from 1990s driving research, defined as the fraction of a time window the eyes are more than 80% closed. Know why it beats counting blinks, what a microsleep is, what the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale is, and why 02:00–06:00 is the dangerous window. Being able to cite the origin of your own metric is the difference between a project and a science project.

Unlocks awas/driver/window.py — and every threshold in config/default.yaml
  • PaperDinges & Grace (1998), the PERCLOS validation report — see the reading list.
  • ReadingSearch "Karolinska Sleepiness Scale" — a one-page 1–9 scale. We ask for it at trip start and end.
  • ReadingSearch "circadian rhythm drowsy driving crash statistics" — the time-of-day pattern is the strongest single predictor in our Phase 4 model.
  • ReadingSearch "standard deviation of lane position drowsiness" — why vehicle behaviour is evidence, not just cameras.
04

Machine learning, and the one concept that makes our numbers believable

~3 weeks

Supervised classification, training and validation, overfitting, and metrics beyond accuracy. But the concept to understand above all others is data leakage, and specifically why we split the dataset by subject rather than by image. MRL has 37 people with hundreds of near-identical photos each. Split randomly and the same eyes appear in training and testing, and you report 99% that means nothing. Split by person and you get our 98.6% on seven people the model has never seen — a number that survives a hostile question.

Unlocks training/mrl.py (split_by_subject), training/baselines.py, training/train_eyestate.py
  • CourseKaggle Learn: Intro to ML then Intermediate ML — the second contains a dedicated Data Leakage lesson. Do that lesson twice.
  • CourseGoogle ML Crash Course — free, well-paced, good on overfitting and generalisation.
  • Video3Blue1Brown — Neural Networks. Four videos. The best intuition available anywhere for what a network is actually doing.
  • VideoStatQuest — precision, recall, ROC and confusion matrices, explained slowly and correctly.
  • DocsPyTorch tutorials — "Learn the Basics". Then read training/model.py; it is only 50 lines.
  • Coursefast.ai Practical Deep Learning — free, top-down, excellent. Bigger commitment; start it after the competition.
05

State machines and why this project has 176 tests

~4 days

A state machine is a set of states, the rules for moving between them, and nothing else. Ours decides whether the vehicle is parked, moving, at a light or in a jam, and it is pure — no camera, no clock, no model. That purity is why it can be tested exhaustively, and being able to explain that design choice is worth more to a judge than any single feature.

Unlocks awas/core/machine.py, tests/test_state_machine.py
  • ReadingSearch "finite state machine explained" — the concept is genuinely small. An hour is enough.
  • ReadingSearch "pure function side effects" — then find every place machine.py avoids reading the clock.
  • ExerciseBreak something on purpose. Change a threshold in config/default.yaml, run the tests, and read which ones fail and why.

07

How research is actually judged

~1 week

This is a research competition, not a science fair, and the difference is that a claim must be falsifiable. Learn what a hypothesis is, what a control condition is, what an ablation is, and why effect size matters more than a p-value when your sample is small — which yours will be. Our §6 already has hypotheses H1–H3 written this way; understand them well enough to argue for them.

Unlocks docs/00-master-plan.md §6 — the evaluation protocol
  • PaperKeshav, "How to Read a Paper" — three pages, the three-pass method. Read it before any other paper.
  • ReadingCenter for Open Science — preregistration. Deciding your analysis before collecting data is what makes a small study credible.
  • VideoStatQuest again — p-values, confidence intervals, and what they do not mean.
  • ReadingSearch "within-subject vs between-subject design" — why we can run a meaningful study with very few participants.

Six questions a judge will ask

If each student can answer these without hedging, the comprehension goal is met. The answers already exist in the repo — the work is knowing where.

“Isn't this just detecting closed eyes?”
No — a single closed frame is a blink. We measure PERCLOS over a 60-second window, separate blinks from microsleeps by duration, and require a level to hold for three consecutive updates before escalating. See awas/driver/window.py.
“Why should we believe 98.6%?”
Because it is measured on seven people the model never saw during training, and because we report the logistic-regression baseline next to it — 86.8%. The gap is what shows the network earned its place. A random per-image split would have looked better and meant nothing.
“Isn't talking to a drowsy driver a distraction?”
That is exactly what hypothesis H3 tests. Conversation is suppressed when the workload estimate is high, emergencies use pre-recorded audio that never waits for an AI, and we measure lane position to detect if we are making things worse. See master plan §5.2.
“What happens when the camera is blocked?”
Confidence drops, the level is capped at 3 instead of 4, and the driver is told once. A lost face is never counted as a closed eye — there is a test named for it. See §5.4 and tests/test_window.py.
“Why not just use a fixed EAR threshold?”
Because eye shape varies two- to threefold between people, so one threshold is too twitchy for one driver and too deaf for the next. Run demo_calibration — it shows the same true drowsiness read correctly for both drivers on a personal scale and wrongly for both on a fixed one.
“What is actually new here?”
Measuring whether an intervention worked — time-to-relapse per intervention type. No system on the market does this, because none of them talk to the driver and therefore none can measure the reply. See docs/01-architecture-v3.md §2.

The five papers worth reading

Search each title — all five are freely available. Read them with Keshav's three-pass method rather than start to finish.

Real-Time Eye Blink Detection using Facial Landmarks Soukupová & Čech, 2016 Where EAR comes from. Note that their best result used a classifier over a window of EAR values, not a bare threshold — the authors did not recommend the version most tutorials copy.
PERCLOS: A Valid Psychophysiological Measure of Alertness Dinges & Grace, 1998 · report FHWA-MCRT-98-006 The origin of the measure at the centre of Layer 1. Originally scored by humans watching video, which is why it is a sound measure independently of any automatic detector.
Real-time Facial Surface Geometry from Monocular Video on Mobile GPUs Kartynnik et al., 2019 The model behind MediaPipe Face Mesh — the thing producing our 478 landmarks. Worth knowing that our "geometric" pipeline sits on top of a neural network.
Subjective and objective sleepiness in the active individual Åkerstedt & Gillberg, 1990 The Karolinska Sleepiness Scale. Our only ground-truth label for how a driver actually felt, and free to collect because the system can simply ask.
How to Read a Paper S. Keshav, 2007 Three pages that will save you weeks. Read this one first, then use its method on the four above.

What not to learn yet

Time before the deadline is the scarcest thing you have. These are all worth knowing eventually and none of them will help you now.

  • Transformers and large language models. Phase 1 uses written templates, not a language model. Fascinating, irrelevant until Milestone 4.
  • Docker, Kubernetes, cloud deployment. The whole point is that this runs offline on a small board.
  • Training big models. Ours has 23,538 parameters and trains in two minutes on a laptop. Scale is not the problem you have.
  • Web frameworks. The fleet dashboard is Phase 3 and it is a mockup for now.
  • Writing your own face detector. MediaPipe took Google years. Use it and spend your effort where you can actually be better than the alternatives.
